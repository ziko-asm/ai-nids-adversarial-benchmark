Adversarial ML · network intrusion detection

The same detector, the same attack, two datasets. Opposite conclusions.

An MLP and a 1D-CNN are trained to flag malicious network connections, then attacked by an adversary who reshapes its own traffic. On KDD Cup 1999 they look robust. On the more recent UNSW-NB15 they break, and the standard defence collapses.

By Ziya Azizov, ML security engineer Code and full results on GitHub Companion: GraphGuard IoT

Why the datasets disagree

Attacks in UNSW-NB15 sit far closer to normal traffic

L∞ distance from 2,000 test attacks to the nearest of 40,000 benign training records, in the model's input space. A detector cannot be robust at budget ε for an attack whose nearest benign record is closer than ε.

    Explanations ·

    What the undefended detectors rely on

    Integrated Gradients on 2,000 attack records: share of total attribution per input. Features marked frozen cannot be changed by the constrained attacker.

    MLP

    1D-CNN

    Detection rate by attack family, MLP, constrained PGD at ε = 0.5

    Method

    Built to be argued with

    • Exact duplicates removed before splitting: 494,021 KDD rows shrink to 145,586. In UNSW-NB15, 828 rows whose identical features carry both labels are dropped.
    • Stratified 60 / 20 / 20 split, 3 seeds, early stopping on validation F1, standardisation fitted on the training split only.
    • FGSM and PGD-20 white-box attacks; gradient-boosted trees attacked by transfer.
    • Every number on this page is read from the committed results.json files.
    Limits

    What this does not show

    • Adversarially trained models show gradient masking, so their PGD numbers are upper bounds. Gradient-free or adaptive attacks are the next step.
    • The constrained model freezes fields and bounds values, but a perturbed record may still not match any real packet trace.
    • UNSW-NB15 has a TTL shortcut: a depth-3 tree on sttl alone reaches about 0.82 accuracy.
    • Evasion only. Poisoning the training data is not covered.
